\title{The Execution Floor: Budget Allocation, Not Test-Time Scaffolding or Search,

\begin{abstract}
For a fixed whole-proof theorem prover, we ask whether test-time interventions --- agentic scaffolding
(premise retrieval, failure memory, an LLM reviewer, tactic-skeleton hints, forced approach diversity),
additional search, or additional per-problem budget --- can move solve rate beyond what raw sampling
already buys at a given token budget $B$. Across two independently-trained provers (Goedel-Prover-V2-8B,
DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B), two benchmarks (in-distribution miniF2F, out-of-distribution ProofNet\#), we
find that none of these test-time levers move solve rate. A pre-registered, causally interventional test
(forcing approach diversity on the fully-trapped problem core) confirms the mechanism directly ---
diversity rises 42--70\% under a budget-matched manipulation check, yet solves stay flat and output
quality mildly degrades: \textbf{approach discovery is not the bottleneck; within-approach execution
depth is}. We further test two training-time interventions on this same bottleneck: closing-likelihood
SFT finds a diagnostic exposure-bias signature rather than a fix, and a lightweight post-hoc GRPO probe
finds no gain on a policy that, by its own KL and reward-flatness signature, was already close to
saturated --- consistent with, not contradicted by, an independent report of the same behavior on the
same base model in the literature. A third, stronger training intervention (a lab-scale full
Base$\to$SFT$\to$RL pipeline on two matched lineages) was attempted but its result is withdrawn here: an
independent audit found a verifier bug specific to that phase's completion format that mechanically
produces a 0\% outcome regardless of underlying model quality, so we report the finding and its cause
rather than a number the audit shows cannot be trusted. The one lever that reliably moves outcomes at
fixed compute is \emph{how} a budget is allocated across a batch of \emph{different} problems, not any
change to any single problem's search or training: a realizable, mechanism-informed allocation policy
saves $\sim$30\% of compute at 90\% of full-budget accuracy on one model (Goedel$\times$ProofNet\#, the
larger and more consistently positive of two point estimates), though a per-problem bootstrap CI on
that same estimate crosses zero and the second model's result is weaker and also inconclusive at the
95\% level --- reported as the strongest lever we found, not as a settled positive result. We position this
as a definitive-negative result, narrowly scoped to test-time scaffolding and to the training
interventions we could trust, against an active literature that treats scaffolding and test-time compute
as reliable levers, and as a methodological case study in verifier auditing, symmetric skepticism of both
surprising positives and surprising nulls, and causal (not just correlational) mechanism testing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language models fine-tuned for formal theorem proving (``whole-proof'' provers that emit a
complete tactic proof in one generation, as opposed to step-by-step interactive search) have improved
rapidly, and a growing body of work treats \emph{test-time compute} --- more sampling, agentic
scaffolding, retrieval-augmented context, search over partial proof states --- as a reliable lever for
squeezing more solves out of a fixed model. We ask a narrower, falsifiable question: for two
independently-trained whole-proof provers, at a fixed per-problem token budget $B$, what actually moves
solve rate?

We find an \emph{execution floor}: search-time scaffolding, closing-tactic libraries, and additional
budget on already-attempted problems all fail to move solve rate, and a pre-registered causal test
localizes why --- the bottleneck is within-approach execution depth, not approach discovery, so no
lever that only changes \emph{which} approach is tried should be expected to help. We test this same
bottleneck against two training-time interventions we could fully trust (closing-likelihood SFT and a
lightweight post-hoc RL probe): neither moves it, though the RL probe's own signature (near-zero
policy KL, flat reward) supports a narrower reading --- an already-saturated policy, not a proven
capacity ceiling --- corroborated by an independent report of the same behavior on the same base model
elsewhere in the literature. A third, stronger training intervention we attempted (full lab-scale
Base$\to$SFT$\to$RL, two matched lineages) is withdrawn rather than reported: our own audit found a
phase-specific verifier bug that mechanically forces a 0\% outcome independent of model quality, so we
report the bug and its mechanism, not a number we know cannot be trusted. The one exception to the
floor is not a model or scaffold change at all, but a \emph{policy} question: given a fixed total
compute budget, how should it be split across a batch of different problems. This paper reports the
floor --- scoped to the interventions we could actually test and trust, not as a claim that nothing
could ever move it --- as the headline finding, with the allocation result as a constructive
counterpoint scoped to what is currently confirmed (Section~\ref{sec:allocation}).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A cross-model (2 provers), cross-benchmark (in-distribution / OOD) replication of the
    budget$\to$solve relationship and its saturation asymmetry (Section~\ref{sec:passb}).
  \item A one-factor-at-a-time ablation of six agentic scaffolding components showing none is a net
    positive lever, and that two are actively harmful out-of-distribution (Section~\ref{sec:scaffolding}).
  \item A four-part mechanism account (diversity collapse onto a deep-reasoning execution floor, on
    which the one retrieval variant we tested empirically fails) culminating in a \emph{pre-registered,
    causally interventional} test that confirms the mechanism directly rather than by correlation alone
    (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
  \item An exhaustion sweep across five further candidate levers --- hammer/SMT tactics, more budget on
    already-trapped problems, closing-likelihood SFT, lightweight post-hoc RL, and verified-state
    re-grounding --- all null, extending the floor from search-time to training-time interventions
    (Section~\ref{sec:exhaustion}). A sixth, stronger candidate (full-pipeline lab-scale RL across two
    matched training lineages) was attempted but is \textbf{withdrawn, not reported as null}: an
    independent audit found a phase-specific verifier bug that forces a 0\% outcome mechanically,
    regardless of model quality (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) --- we report the bug and its causal
    mechanism as a methods contribution in its own right, not the invalidated number.
  \item A constructive counterpoint: compute-optimal budget allocation across problems, the strongest
    lever we found, reported with a paired per-problem bootstrap CI rather than a 3-8-seed std --- the
    more conservative accounting this CI provides means we report the result as the best point estimate
    available, not yet a statistically settled positive on either model (Section~\ref{sec:allocation}).
  \item A methodology contribution: a verifier-soundness audit protocol, and the discipline of applying
    the same base-rate skepticism to surprising nulls as to surprising positives, which caught two
    verifier bugs in opposite directions on the same experimental phase --- one that would have shipped
    a spurious ``training moves the floor'' positive, and a later one that would have shipped a
    spurious ``training never moves the floor'' 0\% null (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).
  \item Two transferable methods findings for anyone building similar agentic evaluation or refinement
    pipelines, not just a caveat on our own numbers: (i) \textbf{pass@\emph{budget} is not
    pass@\emph{N}} --- the two metrics diverge in a budget-dependent, not fixed-offset way, and we give
    the attempts-per-budget table needed to convert between them (Section~\ref{sec:setup}); (ii) a Lean
    elaboration-heartbeat setting that is lenient at verification time but was, for months, effectively
    stricter during a refinement loop's own intermediate steps silently injected false-negative
    feedback into 17.8\% of miniF2F refine steps --- a warning for any refinement-loop builder to audit
    this specific failure mode before trusting their loop's feedback signal (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Models.} Two independently-trained whole-proof provers: Goedel-Prover-V2-8B
(\texttt{Goedel-LM/Goedel-Prover-V2-8B}, Lean~v4.9.0-rc1 + mathlib4) and DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B
(\texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B}, Lean~v4.9.0 + standard mathlib4). Pinned model
revisions, Lean toolchains, and mathlib commits are given in Appendix~\ref{app:pins}.

\textbf{Benchmarks.} miniF2F-test (244 audited problems; competition-style, in-distribution for both
provers) and ProofNet\# (186 test; undergraduate-level, out-of-distribution). DeepSeek-Prover-V2
inherits Goedel's protocol exactly --- identical canonical statement sets, budgets, and agent
configuration, with only the model and Lean pin changed --- so any cross-model difference is
attributable to the model, not the harness.

\textbf{Verifier.} Lean itself (REPL backend) is the sole source of truth for ``solved.'' We treat the
verifier as a first-class audited artifact, not an assumed-correct oracle; Section~\ref{sec:methodology}
details two rounds of verifier-soundness bugs found and fixed over the project's lifetime, and their
measured blast radius on every reported number in this paper.

\textbf{Budget and variance protocol.} Token budgets $B \in \{2\text{k}, 8\text{k}, 32\text{k},
128\text{k}\}$; every headline number is 3 seeds minimum, reported mean $\pm$ seed-std. A mean delta
under $\sim$1 baseline-$\sigma$ is treated as noise throughout (the ``noise bar''); component-vs-baseline
comparisons use paired flips (per-(problem, seed) solved/unsolved transitions), not mean deltas, since
at $n=3$ seeds mean deltas can hide gains-equal-losses churn that a flip table exposes directly
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, lesson 1).

\textbf{pass@\emph{B} is not pass@\emph{N}.} We deliberately report pass@\emph{budget} (solved within a
cumulative token ledger shared across propose and refine calls for one problem) rather than the
test-time-compute literature's usual pass@\emph{N} (solved within $N$ independent, unrefined samples).
The two are not interchangeable, and the gap is budget-dependent, not a fixed offset: at $B$=2k the
\emph{median} cell across all four (model, benchmark) baselines completes zero full propose attempts
--- the first generation call itself does not finish within budget for most cells, so the 2k point
measures whether a truncated partial attempt happened to already contain a complete proof, not whether
the model got a fair single try. Attempts-per-cell only approach one full independent sample by $B$=8k
(mean propose count 0.82--0.87 across the four baselines) and keep climbing well past $N$=1 at every
larger budget we report (mean propose count at 128k: 1.94/2.22 miniF2F, 4.71/5.85 ProofNet\#, Goedel/
DeepSeek respectively; full table in \texttt{results/phase0/ATTEMPTS\_PER\_BUDGET\_TABLE.md}). Any
claim that maps this paper's budget axis onto a literature pass@$N$ number should use this table, not
assume $B$$\leftrightarrow$$N$ parity.

advantage is deeper within-approach execution, not more approach diversity: every DeepSeek-only win is
on an opening Goedel also tried but could not close, and on shared misses DeepSeek diversifies
\emph{less} (3.08 vs 4.07 distinct openings) yet elaborates \emph{deeper} (median deepest step 29 vs
24). The lever that separates these two models is the model itself --- its training distribution and
recipe --- not search-time scaffolding, which is exactly the question the rest of this paper
interrogates directly.

\section{Agentic scaffolding is null (Phase 1)}
\label{sec:scaffolding}

We ablate six scaffolding components one-factor-at-a-time against a no-frills whole-proof + refinement
baseline (max\_iters 4, alloc\_split 0.5, all Phase 1 components off): BM25 premise retrieval ($k$=8),
a failure-memory buffer, an LLM reviewer over Lean-failed attempts, tactic-skeleton hints, and two
budget-allocation variants.

\textbf{miniF2F: no component clears the noise bar, and one case shows why a single run's CI is not
enough.} Retrieval looked like a mover in an initial pass (+3.4pp@8k) but did not replicate under
paired-flip inspection in an independent re-run at the same nominal budget (+2.2/+0.7/$-0.8$pp at
2k/8k/32k; gains $\approx$ losses at every budget --- BM25 context perturbs sampling without injecting
usable premises). \textbf{This is a genuinely informative surprise from moving to equivalence-style
bounds (Section~\ref{sec:setup}): a paired per-problem bootstrap 95\% CI computed on the initial run
alone is [+0.82pp, +6.15pp] --- entirely positive, clearing zero with room to spare.} Taken on its own,
that CI would read as a confirmed positive effect. It is not, and the replication run's own bootstrap
CI (same method, independent generation campaign, same seeds; \texttt{results/RETRIEVAL\_REPLICATION\_CI.md})
makes this a quantified contrast rather than a single-point disagreement: 2k
$[+0.00,+4.51]$pp, 8k $[-1.78,+3.14]$pp, 32k $[-2.73,+1.09]$pp --- all three cross or touch zero, and
the 8k interval does not overlap the original run's $[+0.82,+6.15]$pp at all. \textbf{The lesson is
explicit and belongs in the methods claim, not buried}: a per-problem bootstrap CI is a real
improvement over a 3-seed std --- it uses the right resampling unit and gives an honest bound on
within-run sampling uncertainty --- but it cannot capture \emph{run-to-run} (generation-campaign)
variance, which here is large enough to move a tight, entirely-positive CI to one that crosses zero.
This generalizes beyond this one comparison: any paper reporting a bootstrap CI over a single
generation run is bounding within-run variance only, not the uncertainty a reader would actually want.
Where an independent replication exists, we report both and trust the replication; where it does not
(every other cell below), we report the bootstrap bound as the best available uncertainty estimate and
flag it as bounded by within-run variance only.

precisely when the model is already out of its depth. \textbf{No Phase~1 component is a net-positive
lever}, and the failure mode gets worse, not better, on the harder benchmark.

\section{Mechanism: why scaffolding cannot help, tested causally}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Section~\ref{sec:scaffolding} shows \emph{what}; this section explains \emph{why}, by mining the
on-disk attempt traces and then testing the resulting explanation with a pre-registered intervention,
not just correlation. Every finding below holds on both provers $\times$ both benchmarks.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{F1}] \textbf{Diversity collapse.} On never-solved problems, the model tries only
    $\sim$1.8 distinct opening tactics across 18--33 attempts --- it loops on $\sim$2 approaches
    regardless of budget.
  \item[\textbf{F2}] \textbf{Failure taxonomy.} By our classifier's \emph{terminal} label (the single
    most severe failure mode per cell, across its full attempt history), unsolved attempts fail at deep
    reasoning (94--100\% \texttt{reasoning\_deep}) and almost never terminate on a missing lemma
    ($\sim$0--1\% \texttt{knowledge\_hallucinated\_lemma}). Because the terminal label reports only the
    last, most severe failure, it undercounts non-terminal premise/knowledge errors encountered earlier
    in a cell's history: on ProofNet\#, an unknown-identifier error is \emph{encountered} in 51.9\% of
    attempts even though it is almost never the terminal cause. \textbf{BM25 premise retrieval fails
    net of its context-perturbation cost (F2$'$, Section~\ref{sec:scaffolding}) despite roughly half
    of ProofNet\# attempts hitting an unresolvable identifier} --- a sharper finding than the original
    reading: retrieval had a real problem to solve and still did not solve it, rather than facing a
    distribution with nothing to retrieve. Whether a stronger, neural retriever fares differently on
    that same encountered-but-unsolved population is untested here (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
  \item[\textbf{F3}] \textbf{Capability floor.} Unsolved attempts reach a median deepest step of
    26--53 and almost never stall at step 1: the model makes real progress into a proof but cannot
    \emph{close} it.
  \item[\textbf{F4}] \textbf{No easy subfield.} ProofNet\# solve rates are 21--33\% across every
    subfield --- there is no soft target a smarter router could farm.
  \item[\textbf{F5}] \textbf{Late solves re-use the same approach (correlational).} When a hard problem
    is finally solved late (after $\geq$3 attempts), it comes from re-sampling the \emph{same} approach,
    not a newly explored one (0\% new opening). This predicts that forcing approach diversity will not
    help, but is survivor-conditioned and cannot establish causation on its own.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{F6 / Step C: the interventional test (centerpiece)}
\label{sec:stepc}

We built an approach-conditioned diversity-injection component --- lists the distinct openings already
tried, asks explicitly for a fundamentally different approach --- and ran it on each model$\times$
benchmark's \emph{trapped core} (problems unsolved by every seed at the 128k baseline) at 8k/32k, 3
seeds, all four cells. We pre-registered the prediction: diversity rises, solves stay flat.

\textbf{The manipulation check passed}: budget-matched (@32k, attempt counts held fixed), distinct
openings per attempt rose 42--70\% ($\sim$1.0--1.3 $\to$ $\sim$1.7--1.8) --- the intervention genuinely
fired, not just nominally. \textbf{Solves stayed null}: trapped pass@8k/32k $\approx$ 0 everywhere
(Goedel miniF2F 0.6/1.2\%, Goedel ProofNet\# 0/0.7\%, DeepSeek both 0/0\%) --- exactly 5 genuine
verified flips across all four arms (0 on DeepSeek), within seed-std. \textbf{Output quality mildly
degraded}: last-attempt failure modes shift from \texttt{reasoning\_deep} (38--88\%) to majority
\texttt{formalization\_syntax} (62--74\%) plus \texttt{loophole\_sorry} (23--36\%); soundness-relevant
rates rise ($\sim$3$\times$ loophole, $\sim$1.5--2$\times$ syntax). Pushed for novelty, the model leaves
its competent manifold --- but every degraded attempt is caught by the verifier (0 false solves), so
result soundness is intact and the intervention is measurably counterproductive, not merely neutral.

This closes F5's correlational gap with a causal experiment on two independent provers:
\textbf{approach discovery is not the bottleneck; within-approach execution depth (the F2/F3 reasoning
floor) is.} No downstream lever that only changes \emph{which} approach is tried, without changing the
model's capacity to execute a chosen approach to completion, should be expected to move solve rate ---
which sets up the exhaustion sweep in Section~\ref{sec:exhaustion}.

\section{Exhaustion sweep: five further candidate levers, all null}
\label{sec:exhaustion}

Having isolated the mechanism causally, we exhaust the remaining candidate levers a reviewer might
propose, compressed here (full detail in the phase result docs cited inline).

\textbf{Hammer/SMT closing tactics (Phase 3).} A portfolio of hammer/SMT-style closing tactics against
the trapped core: 0/119 newly solved. Rules out ``the model just needs a stronger closing-tactic
library'' as cheaply as Section~\ref{sec:scaffolding} ruled out scaffolding.

\textbf{More of the same budget (Phase 5).} Extending already-trapped cells past 128k tokens (more of
the \emph{same} budget on the \emph{same} problems): $\sim$0 new solves (Goedel 1, DeepSeek 0). Budget
helps only when reallocated \emph{across} different problems (Section~\ref{sec:allocation}), not by
adding more of it to a problem already known to be hard --- a fourth, independent confirmation of the
execution floor.

\textbf{Closing-likelihood SFT (Phase 6, Stage A/B).} Fine-tuning to maximize conditional likelihood on
verified proof closings is a two-model null, but a diagnostically useful one: near-zero teacher-forced
loss on the closing, yet the same model still fails when it has to \emph{generate} its own path there
autoregressively --- an exposure-bias signature. This reframes the open question from ``does the model
know how to close a proof'' (yes) to ``why does it drift away from a closeable state during its own
free-running generation'' (open). Paired per-problem bootstrap CIs (both models, both benchmarks, 3
seeds; \texttt{results/EQUIVALENCE\_BOUNDS.md}) sharpen this from a uniform ``flat'' read: generic RFT
(Stage A) CIs are entirely negative on all four model$\times$benchmark combinations (it actively hurts,
consistent with prior reporting), and closing-targeted SFT (Stage B) is a clean null on three of four
(DeepSeek miniF2F/ProofNet\# and Goedel miniF2F all cross zero) but is entirely negative on Goedel
ProofNet\# ($-1.97$pp, 95\% CI $[-3.76,-0.54]$pp) --- a small but statistically real harm on that one
combination, not indistinguishable-from-flat as previously stated. This does not change the headline
(closing-targeted SFT still does not lift pass@B on any combination we tested, so the pivot to Stage C
stands), but the honest per-combination reading is ``null-to-mildly-harmful,'' not uniformly flat. The
one harmed combination is not arbitrary: Goedel$\times$ProofNet\# is the weakest of the four base cells
(14.2\% pass@32k, vs.\ 17.6\%/61.5\%/70.4\% for the other three), i.e.\ exactly where closing-targeted
SFT has the least valid signal to fine-tune on and the most room to narrow an already-thin output
distribution --- the same mechanism as Stage A's uniform harm, at smaller amplitude: likelihood-based
post-hoc adaptation degrades the generation distribution, and the degradation is largest where the base
model was weakest to begin with.

\textbf{Lightweight post-hoc RL (Phase 6, Stage C).} A GRPO probe (LoRA $r$=16, DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B,
80 steps, direct outcome reward against the Lean verifier) found no gain: held-out pass@1 moved
$0.586 \to 0.570$ ($\Delta = -1.6$pp), training reward flat across all 80 steps and mean KL never
exceeded 0.0021 --- the policy barely moved at all, not that it moved and failed. Manipulation checks
confirm no reward-hacking or diversity collapse, so the flat reward is not a masked artifact. We read
this as \textbf{consistent with an already-saturated policy on this specific checkpoint}, not as
evidence of a general RL capacity ceiling: Leanabell-Prover-V2 independently reports vanilla
outcome-reward RL producing no gain on DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B specifically, attributing it to an
already-saturated policy with a flat pass@$k$ slope, while a method-dependent variant (GAR) moves the
same base model modestly \citep{leanabell-prover-v2}. ``This model is RL-saturated; post-hoc RL gives
small, method-dependent gains'' is what this probe plus the corroborating literature support; ``there
is a capacity ceiling'' is a stronger claim than a single small-LoRA probe can carry on its own, and we
do not make it.

\textbf{Verified-state re-grounding (Phase 7).} Tests the Stage~B exposure-bias hypothesis directly and
training-independently: force continuation from a \emph{verified} intermediate Lean state rather than
free-running generation. Both re-grounding modes tested, both models: null. Re-grounding alone does not
unlock the trapped core.

\textbf{Compositional decomposition cannot be elicited (WS6).} If execution depth, not approach
discovery, is the bottleneck, a natural lever is to make each execution step shorter: decompose the
goal into \texttt{have} sub-lemmas, prove each independently, then splice the verified subproofs back
together. We built the full pipeline (structural sketch check, per-subgoal proving, byte-level
composition) and pre-registered a decision rule requiring at least one structurally valid decomposition
before committing GPU budget to the trapped-core array. Across five independent prompting attempts on
two models (four rounds on Goedel-Prover-V2-8B --- zero-shot, revised prompt, larger budget, few-shot
with an explicit anti-degenerate instruction --- plus one round on DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B using the
already-matured prompt), \emph{we never observed a genuine multi-\texttt{have}
\texttt{sorry}-deferred decomposition on a trapped problem across these five rounds} --- this is a
strong existence claim about a failure mode repeatedly observed, not a measured rate from a systematic
sweep. Both models consistently either attempted a full inline solution with zero \texttt{sorry}s, or
wrote one or more genuine intermediate \texttt{have}s but then closed with a bare \texttt{sorry} rather
than a real tactic --- DeepSeek got closer on this second failure mode, producing three genuine
sub-facts before still punting on the close. That last detail matters for what the finding actually
locates: DeepSeek's willingness to \emph{state} three separate correct-looking intermediate facts shows
the failure is not an inability to identify subgoals, but specifically an inability (or unwillingness)
to \emph{defer} them with \texttt{sorry} and treat them as separable proof obligations --- whole-proof
training appears to remove the deferral mode, not the decomposition vocabulary. We spent $\sim$2.3
GPU-h across all five rounds rather than the full array, per the pre-registered rule. Paired with
Phase~7's tactic-level re-grounding null above, this is a second, independent probe at a different
decomposition granularity giving the same answer: these frozen whole-proof provers have no accessible
mode for honest decomposition with acknowledged gaps, via prompting alone. That is a structural claim
about the model class, not just a null on this
intervention --- it is consistent with, and helps explain, why current agentic systems that
\emph{train} for decomposition see gains scaffolding never bought a frozen whole-proof prover here.
Whether targeted fine-tuning could elicit it remains open (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\textbf{Full-pipeline, lab-scale RL (Phase 8) --- withdrawn.} We attempted the strongest remaining
candidate: not a lightweight post-hoc probe but a lab's own full Base$\to$SFT$\to$RL pipeline, on two
independently matched training lineages (DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 and Leanabell-Prover). An initial pass
surfaced and fixed four harness bugs and reported a corrected 0.0\% pass@$B$ at every training stage,
every budget, both lineages. A subsequent, independent audit (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) found a
fifth bug specific to this phase: the verifier's \texttt{no\_goal} soundness gate compared a raw
extracted completion against a regex, rather than the backend's actually-assembled/compiled source ---
for continuation-style templates (both Phase~8 lineages, by construction), that comparison is
structurally always empty regardless of correctness, so \texttt{no\_goal} fired on every attempt
independent of whether the underlying proof was valid. This is not a small-magnitude correction like
Task B's heartbeat fix: the affected completions from the original sweep were not retained, the bug
mechanically explains a 0.0\%-everywhere outcome regardless of true model capability, and published
numbers for these exact model checkpoints sit 50--60 points above 0. \textbf{We therefore withdraw
Phase~8's headline rather than report a number the audit shows cannot be trusted either way}; a
re-run under the fixed verifier is future work, not reported here. This leaves the RL question
resting on Phase~6 Stage~C's lightweight post-hoc probe alone (above), whose claim we state narrowly
there: no gain from a small LoRA GRPO probe on an already near-saturated policy, not a general
capacity ceiling.

\section{The constructive counterpoint: compute-optimal budget allocation}
\label{sec:allocation}

The one lever that moved anything in this project is not a change to any single problem's search or
training at all, but a \emph{policy} for how a fixed total token budget is split across a batch of
\emph{different} problems.

\textbf{Positioning against adaptive-allocation prior art.} Compute-optimal allocation across a batch of
problems is not a new idea: constrained-optimization framings of test-time compute allocation, and
training-free difficulty proxies for routing budget toward easier or more promising instances, both have
established prior art \todo{cite constrained-optimization test-time-compute allocation and training-free
difficulty-proxy routing papers, full citation pass pending}. What is new here is the \emph{regime}: our
predictor is verifier-grounded (trained and evaluated purely on Lean-verified attempt traces, not a
proxy score), and it operates on a \textbf{rare-winnable} population --- $\sim$80\% of ProofNet\# cells
never solve at any budget, so the responsive population the policy can actually move is thin, and the
oracle-realizable gap (Section~\ref{sec:allocation}, below) is best read as a \emph{predictability cost}
specific to this regime: with so few winnable-but-expensive cells, telling them apart from
never-winnable ones this early is intrinsically harder than in a regime where most cells are eventually
solvable and the question is only how much budget each one needs. This is a different problem shape
than difficulty-routing over a mostly-solvable pool, and we position our contribution as evidence for
\emph{this} regime specifically, not as a general claim that allocation beats other adaptive-compute
methods everywhere.

On ProofNet\# ($\sim$80\% of cells never solve, so uniform allocation pours full budget into a
provably-trapped core), a \emph{realizable} during-run policy --- abandon cells a logistic predictor
flags as trapped at a decision checkpoint $c^*$, informed by the F2/F3 mechanism (elapsed spend, proof-
depth plateau) --- saves substantial compute at near-equal accuracy on Goedel: \textbf{+26\% $\pm$ 7\%}
saved at 90\% of full-budget accuracy on the original 3 seeds (+25/+18/+34). DeepSeek's original 3-seed
result was weak and fragile by comparison ($-13\% \pm 28\%$, driven by a seed-2 collapse to $-51\%$); an
8-seed power-up (\texttt{results/phase4/perseed.json}, seeds 0--7) resolved this from noise-dominated
toward a small positive: \textbf{+10\% $\pm$ 24\%}, no single catastrophic seed, but still crossing zero
within 1$\sigma$ --- \textbf{one-model-robust and model-dependent, not a confirmed general property, even
with the added seeds}.

\textbf{From seed-std to a paired per-problem bootstrap.} A 1$\sigma$-seed-std gate is itself
barely estimated at $n$=3--8 seeds, and treats each seed's pooled-population statistic as one
independent draw when the real information is at the level of $\sim$186--244 individual problems. We
therefore also report a paired per-problem bootstrap 95\% CI (2000+ resamples of problem identities,
each resampled problem's cells pooled across every seed it appears in, decision checkpoint and OOF
predictor held fixed, $n$=3000 resamples): Goedel$\times$ProofNet\# point estimate \textbf{+29.7\%},
95\% CI \textbf{[$-$42.4\%, +57.8\%]}; DeepSeek$\times$ProofNet\# point estimate \textbf{+16.1\%}, 95\%
CI \textbf{[$-$14.8\%, +41.8\%]} (\texttt{scripts/phase4\_bootstrap\_ci.py},
\texttt{results/phase4/bootstrap\_ci.json}). The point estimates match the pooled headline numbers
above almost exactly, but \textbf{both CIs cross zero} --- substantially wider than the seed-std
framing implied, including for Goedel, whose 3-seed std ($\pm$7\%) made it look far more settled than
the per-problem resampling distribution supports. This is an honest consequence of resampling at the
problem level (186 problems) rather than averaging three already-aggregated seed statistics, and it
changes how we scope the claim: \textbf{Goedel's point estimate is the larger and more consistently
positive of the two, but neither model's realizable-policy gain clears zero at the 95\% level under
this more conservative accounting} --- ``one-model-robust'' as previously stated overstates the
Goedel result's own settledness, even though it remains the stronger of the two point estimates by a
wide margin and the qualitative STRONG-vs-WEAK ordering between the two models is unchanged. Our own post-hoc mechanism analysis
(\texttt{ALLOCATION\_MECHANISM.md}) finds DeepSeek's residual fragility is better explained as a
thin-population sampling artifact (fewer solved cells per seed to route budget toward) than a structural
deficit in its population of exploitable cells. We report this result honestly scoped: the win is at a
fractional-accuracy operating point (at 100\% accuracy the policy must keep almost every cell, since the
costliest winnable cells are indistinguishable from trapped ones at the decision checkpoint, so the
saving is $\sim$0 there by construction, not by tuning failure), and efficiency rather than raw
fixed-compute accuracy is the robust axis.

\section{Methodology: auditing the verifier, and symmetric skepticism}
\label{sec:methodology}

Every number in this paper depends on the Lean verifier being sound, so we treat it as a first-class
audited artifact rather than an assumed-correct oracle, across two independent audit rounds.

\textbf{Round 1 (2026-06-14).} Found two verifier holes producing false-positive solves on hard
problems: a truncated generation emitting a bare \texttt{def} preamble that Lean compiles with no goal
(scored solved), and a wedged/cross-talked REPL returning an empty no-error response (scored success).
Both fixed; blast radius measured directly rather than assumed: miniF2F Phase~0 had 0 false positives,
Phase~1 had 6/3124 (0.2\%); only ProofNet\# was materially corrupted (constant full-budget truncation),
and was fully re-run on the fixed verifier.

\textbf{Round 2 (independent audit, 2026-07-10/16).} A separate audit pass, run before any further
experiments were queued, found a further soundness gate bug: the \texttt{no\_goal} check compared the
model's raw extracted completion against a regex, rather than the backend's actually-assembled/compiled
source --- structurally always-failing for continuation-style completions regardless of correctness.
Confirmed, via a taint audit and three independent from-scratch re-derivations that matched the
committed pass@$B$ curve, oracle ceiling, and flip table exactly, to be Phase-8-only and to have zero
effect on any number reported in Sections~\ref{sec:passb}--\ref{sec:allocation}
(\texttt{whole\_proof}, the template family both headline models use, is a no-op case for this gate).
A further, materially small (13/1212 cells, 2.0\% of trapped problems, both models) retroactive
correction was found on re-verifying trapped-core failures under a Lean elaboration-heartbeat setting
introduced after those results were generated; every flipped cell's proof was already present in the
original generation, so the fix only ever widens what counts as solved and the reported floor is, if
anything, a small upward correction, not an overturned result. \todo{fold the 13 corrected cells back
into the affected pass@B curves once flowed --- arithmetic-only, not a new experiment.} This 13/1212
figure is the \emph{final-scoring} blast radius, and understates the setting's live effect on the
refinement loop itself: on miniF2F, 17.8\% of refine steps (both models, 550/3090 Goedel and
718/4046 DeepSeek) were immediately preceded by a spurious Lean heartbeat timeout that the
model then had to react to as if it were a real proof error --- a live trajectory distortion, not
just a scoring artifact, that this paper has not attempted to quantify beyond the terminal-cell flip
count. \textbf{Methods warning for refinement-loop builders:} an elaboration-heartbeat limit that is
lenient at verification time but strict (or absent) during the same run's intermediate refine calls
silently injects false negative feedback into the loop the model is conditioning on --- worth an
explicit audit before trusting any refinement-based agent's feedback signal, independent of whether
the final scoring bug it causes turns out to be small.

\textbf{Lessons carried forward, with sharpest first:} (1)~paired flips beat mean-deltas at $n{=}3$
seeds --- the flip table is what exposed retrieval as churn rather than signal. (2)~A second, harder,
OOD benchmark earns its cost: it corroborated the miniF2F null and surfaced components (retrieval,
front-loaded allocation) that actively hurt, invisible on the easier benchmark. (3)~A second,
independently trained model earns its cost: it turned a single-lab result into a mechanism that
generalizes and surfaced the in-distribution/OOD dichotomy. (4)~Distinguish the manipulation check from
the outcome: Step~C's null is only interpretable because we first confirmed the intervention fired
(budget-matched distinct-opening counts), not raw attempt-confounded diversity. (5)~\textbf{Apply the
same base-rate skepticism to a surprising null as to a surprising positive.} Phase~8's original
Base$<$SFT$<$RL result looked like a genuine, clean finding until it was checked against the models' own
published pass rates and a byte-level prompt diff, correcting it to a 0.0\%-everywhere null --- and
\emph{that} 0.0\%-everywhere null itself looked equally clean (it passed a harness-sanity control on an
unaffected reference model) until a later, independent audit found a second, phase-specific verifier bug
that mechanically forces exactly that outcome regardless of model quality. Two rounds of scrutiny on the
same phase, in opposite directions, both caught real bugs --- the discipline that would catch an
implausibly high number has to be applied again, not retired, once the number looks implausibly low or
implausibly clean; a single sanity control passing is evidence, not proof. (6)~A newly introduced model
configuration exercises code paths existing tests never covered: every Phase~8 structural bug was latent
in code that had shipped, and presumably passed review, for weeks, because no test exercised a template
family other than \texttt{whole\_proof} until Phase~8 needed one at real scale --- add the new case as a
failing test against real data \emph{first}.

\section{Discussion, limitations, and anticipated objections}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{``Your harness is broken.''} Section~\ref{sec:methodology} is not an appendix afterthought: the
Phase~8 bug-hunt (which ultimately led us to withdraw that phase's own headline), the 37/37 and 40/40
harness-sanity controls, and two independent verifier audits are reported in the main text because a
floor result is only as credible as the verifier that produced it --- including the discipline to
retract a phase's own result when the verifier that produced it turns out not to be trustworthy for that
specific case.

\textbf{``Only two models, and the training-lineage claim is thinner than it looks.''} Both headline
models (Section~\ref{sec:passb}) get the full scaffolding ablation and the causal mechanism test.
Training-time interventions are tested more narrowly than we originally attempted: closing-likelihood
SFT (Stage~A/B) is a two-model null on the two headline provers, and the lightweight post-hoc RL probe
(Stage~C) is single-model. We did attempt a stronger, two-lineage full Base$\to$SFT$\to$RL pipeline
(Phase~8) specifically to test training-stage effects beyond a single model, but withdrew its result on
audit grounds (Section~\ref{sec:exhaustion}) rather than let an untrustworthy number stand in for that
claim --- so \textbf{full-pipeline RL across multiple training lineages remains untested here, not
confirmed null}, and is the natural reopening lever for future work.

\textbf{``The execution floor is really just where training-set recall ends.''} A natural alternative
reading of this entire paper, and the most obvious skeptical response to its central claim: perhaps
solved problems are solved because they (or close variants) appear in training data, and the ``floor''
is a memorization boundary, not a capability ceiling. We tested this directly rather than leave it to a
reviewer: using the exact TF-IDF disjointness machinery already built for Phase~6's training-corpus
gate (Section~\ref{sec:exhaustion}), we correlated each miniF2F problem's textual similarity to the
Lean-Workbook training corpus against its trapped/solved status. \textbf{The floor is not where
training-set recall ends}: trapped problems are, if anything, marginally \emph{more} textually similar
to the training corpus than solved problems (mean cosine 0.805 vs.\ 0.743, Mann-Whitney $p$=0.049,
rank-biserial $r$=$-0.17$ --- a small effect, and at $n$=55 vs.\ 189 this test is fragile, so we report
the effect size alongside the $p$-value rather than lean on the sign alone) and have a \emph{higher}
exact-statement-overlap rate (3.6\% vs.\ 1.6\%), not a lower one. If trapped problems were unsolved
because the model had seen nothing like them during training, we would expect the opposite pattern on
both measures. Two things keep this from overreaching into a positive claim: TF-IDF similarity to
Lean-Workbook is a proxy for overlap with \emph{one} training corpus, not a measurement of either
model's actual full pretraining mixture, and a plausible confound is that trapped and training-corpus
problems merely share competition-math surface phrasing without that resemblance translating into an
exploitable memorized proof. The defensible claim is therefore \textbf{no evidence for the
memorization-boundary account, and weak evidence against it} --- consistent with, not contradicted by,
F2/F3's reading of trapped failures as execution-depth failures, not recall gaps
(\texttt{results/phase6/CONTAMINATION\_CORRELATION.md}).

\textbf{``Scaffolding was implemented weakly.''} The Section~\ref{sec:stepc} manipulation check is the
direct answer: the diversity intervention demonstrably fired (42--70\% more distinct openings,
budget-matched) and still did not move solves.

\textbf{``miniF2F is saturated/contaminated.''} This is exactly why ProofNet\# carries the
out-of-distribution claims in this paper; the saturation/still-hungry dichotomy between the two
benchmarks is reported as a finding, not hidden as a limitation.

\textbf{``Your Goedel baseline looks low vs.\ the model's own reported number.''} Our 128k-budget,
3-seed miniF2F baseline is 74.9\% (Table~\ref{tab:passb}), against the model authors' reported 84.6\%.
Union coverage across the 3 original seeds plus the 32-fresh-sample calibration cell on the trapped
core (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}) reaches 195/244 = 79.9\% $\approx$ 80\%, landing between the
authors' number and a third-party reproduction of $\sim$78\% --- a believable band, not a harness
discrepancy, once the sampling regime is matched more closely to what a pass@$N$-style headline
number actually measures. This union figure is explicitly not a clean pass@$N$ run (it mixes 3
original seeds with 32 additional trapped-core-only samples) and we do not report it as a headline;
it exists only to close the calibration question raised during this project's own review process
(\texttt{results/phase0/PASS\_AT\_32\_RECONCILIATION.md}).

\textbf{``Negative results aren't contributions.''} We position this paper directly against an active
test-time-compute and agentic-scaffolding literature that is shipping the same components
(Section~\ref{sec:related}) this paper shows are null or actively harmful for whole-proof theorem
proving specifically, with a causal (not correlational) mechanism account backing the test-time null,
plus a transparently reported instance where we chased that same rigor into withdrawing our own
strongest training-time result rather than reporting an invalidated negative.

\textbf{``The mathlib pin is stale, so `unknown identifier' errors are really a harness artifact.''}
Our verifier is pinned to a fixed mathlib4 snapshot (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), and the prover
does hit unresolved identifiers at a non-trivial rate on ProofNet\# (51.9\% of attempts encounter one,
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), which invites this objection. We checked it directly: of 20 uniformly
sampled unresolved identifier names from ProofNet\# baseline attempts, only 3/20 (15\%) have a
same-named declaration anywhere in a fresh clone of current upstream mathlib4, and even those three
are generic, commonly-reused lemma-name suffixes rather than confirmed matches in the right namespace.
The remaining 17/20, including the single most frequent unresolved name in the corpus
(\texttt{IsGroupHomomorphism}, 27 occurrences), have no match at all --- consistent with the model
targeting an API surface (unbundled \texttt{Is*Hom} predicates) that mathlib4 has since replaced
outright, not merely renamed. This sample leans toward genuine model/API mismatch over pin staleness,
though it does not by itself distinguish training-data mathlib version from harness pin version as the
source of that mismatch.

\textbf{Scope limits.} Collected in one place rather than scattered through the results sections above:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Compositional decomposition, elicited via prompting alone, could not be tested at
    scale on either model} (Section~\ref{sec:exhaustion}) --- both provers consistently fail to produce
    a structurally valid sorry-deferred decomposition, so the trapped-core array was never run. Whether
    a fine-tuned model would decompose successfully remains open.
  \item \textbf{Hammer/SMT (Section~\ref{sec:exhaustion}) tested a same-pin tactic portfolio, not a
    true superposition-calculus hammer} against an external ATP backend; the null rules out the cheaper
    intervention only.
  \item \textbf{Full-pipeline RL across multiple training lineages is untested, not confirmed null}
    (withdrawn on audit grounds, Section~\ref{sec:exhaustion}) --- the natural reopening lever if this
    line of work continues.
  \item DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B's trapped core is calibrated on miniF2F (pass@32, uncapped budget,
    official header: 3/61 recovered, and after checking against the already-known heartbeat-fix flip
    list, only 1/61 is a genuinely new fresh-sampling recovery --- sound, same reading as Goedel's own
    calibration cell) but \textbf{remains uncalibrated on ProofNet\#}, where DeepSeek's trapped-core
    claims rest on Goedel's calibration result carrying over by analogy, not a direct check.
  \item Phase~7's verified-state re-grounding result is ProofNet\#-only, not the full
    cross-model/cross-benchmark protocol used elsewhere in this paper. At 3 seeds, its raw solve count
    (5/450 cells, 4 distinct problems) is statistically indistinguishable from a fresh-resampling
    control run with no state-grounding mechanism at all (2/450 cells, 2 distinct problems; Fisher's
    exact $p=0.45$ cell-level, $p=0.68$ distinct-problem level) --- consistent with, not overturning,
    the single-seed null this paper already reports.
  \item The trapped-core population's resampling-recovery rate is $\geq$11\% at $N$=32 fresh samples
    (Section~\ref{sec:exhaustion}) with the recovery curve not yet flat at that $N$ --- the true
    asymptotic recovery rate under unlimited fresh sampling is not established, only a lower bound.
  \item Mathlib version skew against our $\sim$2024-era pin plausibly accounts for some, but on our
    direct check likely a minority, share of the unresolved-identifier rate (above); we cannot cleanly
    separate ``prover trained against a different mathlib era'' from ``prover hallucinating lemmas that
    never existed in any mathlib version'' with the check we ran.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Open question.} The exposure-bias signature (Section~\ref{sec:exhaustion}, Phase~6 Stage~B) ---
near-zero teacher-forced loss on a verified closing, yet autoregressive failure to reach it --- is this
project's most interesting unresolved thread and is analysis-, not training-, shaped; a drift
characterization is ongoing background work (\texttt{results/phase9/DRIFT.md}) and, if a crisp
signature emerges, a natural follow-up.

\section{Conclusion}

We do not claim there is a hard execution floor that nothing could ever move --- that overclaims, and
the scope limits above name concrete untested axes (a true superposition hammer, full-pipeline
multi-lineage RL, and subgoal decomposition under a training-time rather than prompting-only
elicitation) that could still move it. What we do show, across
two independently trained provers and two benchmarks with an in-distribution/OOD asymmetry: test-time
scaffolding on a frozen whole-proof prover operates on the wrong axis, and a pre-registered causal
intervention confirms directly why --- approach discovery is not the bottleneck, within-approach
execution depth is. Post-hoc adaptation on an already-near-saturated policy does not help either, at the
scale and method we could test and trust; a stronger training-time attempt at the same question is
reported as withdrawn, with its causal mechanism, rather than as an invalidated null. The one lever that
does reliably move outcomes at fixed compute is not a change to any single problem's search or training
at all, but allocation policy across a batch of \emph{different} problems --- the strongest point
estimate we found, larger and more consistently positive on one model than a second, but not a
statistically settled positive on either under a per-problem bootstrap CI, reported honestly as such
rather than rounded up. Alongside these results, two
methods findings --- the gap between pass@budget and the literature's pass@$N$, and a verifier-timeout
setting that silently distorts refinement feedback in a widely-used class of agent loop --- are, in our
view, exactly the kind of transferable, checkable finding that makes a negative-results paper worth
citing rather than worth skipping. We offer this as a narrower, better-supported claim than ``nothing
moves it,'' and as a methodological template --- verifier auditing, causal not correlational mechanism
tests, and symmetric skepticism applied to nulls and positives alike, including our own --- for
negative-result reporting in this area more broadly.
